## What it is for and who reads the output

It turns a founder's or expert's voice notes, fragments and interviews into first-person chapters of a thought-leadership book. The output has two readers. The author reads the drafts and editorial notes and makes decisions. The book's eventual buyers read the finished prose. The book's stated purpose is commercial positioning:

> - **Default requirement**: The book must strengthen category positioning, not just explain ideas competently

## Operating procedure

It has five steps (lines 85-103). (1) Pressure-test the brief: objective, audience, positioning, draft maturity, and contradictions or weak sources. (2) Define chapter intent and a blueprint. (3) Draft in first person, one idea per section. (4) Do a revision pass that removes generic phrasing and adds notes where proof is missing. (5) Deliver a package: a versioned draft, editorial notes, and next-review questions. Each part of the package has a fixed template.

## What it has that the proposed writer lacks

1. Gaps must be shown to the caller, not smoothed over. The proposal sends working reasoning to a separate output. It does not require the writer to report where the evidence ran out:

   > **Editorial Gaps Must Be Visible**: Missing proof, uncertain chronology, or weak logic should be called out directly in notes, not hidden inside polished prose.

   The notes template makes this concrete:

   > - Evidence or sourcing gaps

   > - Decisions needed from the author

2. A third kind of grounding beside evidence and interpretation: a stated assumption.

   > **Trace Claims to Sources**: Every substantial claim should be grounded in source notes, explicit assumptions, or validated references.

   > - Assumptions made

   The proposal sorts sentences into evidenced fact and marked interpretation. It has no category for "true only if X holds, and X is unverified". Assumptions differ from interpretation because the caller can confirm or reject them.

3. Protection of a voice the writer does not own. The proposal assumes the writer's own register:

   > - **Voice Protection**: Preserve the author's personality, rhythm, convictions, and strategic message instead of replacing them with generic AI prose

4. A structure step before prose, and a pressure test of the brief before that:

   > - Build a short blueprint before drafting prose

   > - Surface contradictions, missing context, and weak source material early

5. A cutting rule stated as a test the writer can apply, not an adjective:

   > **One Clear Line of Thought per Section**: If a section tries to do three jobs, split it or cut it.

6. An ending that hands the next step back to the caller in concrete terms:

   > - Propose the exact next revision task instead of vague "let me know" endings

## Where it conflicts with the proposal

- Whose purpose leads. The proposal starts from the reader and what they will do with the document. This file starts from the author's commercial goal. Its reader outcome ("Why the reader should care") serves that goal. For ghostwriting, putting the author first is correct. It is still a different root from the proposal's.
- The specificity rule can lead to invention. This rule pushes toward concrete detail even when the sources are thin:

  > **Specific Beats Abstract**: Use scenes, decisions, tensions, mistakes, and lessons instead of general advice whenever possible.

  Together with "explicit assumptions" as allowed grounding, it lets the writer make up a scene and label it an assumption. The proposal would require the scene to come from the source notes, or to go to the caller as a gap.
- It keeps its own memory. The proposal leaves state to the caller and the brief:

  > - **Memory**: Track the author's voice markers, repeated themes, chapter promises, strategic positioning, and unresolved editorial decisions across iterations

- Its success metrics are judged by the author, which agrees with the proposal's "runs judge, not the writer". It also asks the writer to run a "Strategic Revision Pass" on its own draft. That is self-editing, not self-grading, so it is compatible.
- It has nothing on combining several drafts or on evidence tiers.

## What it gets wrong or what looks untested

- The two rules above pull in opposite directions. "Specific Beats Abstract ... whenever possible" pushes toward concrete detail. "Editorial Gaps Must Be Visible" pushes toward admitting there is none. The file never says which wins when the notes lack the scene. A drafting model will usually lean toward the fluent choice.
- "Trace Claims to Sources" uses "should", and "substantial" is undefined. It has no mechanism: no inline markers, and no list of which claims came from which note.
- The "Memory" line assumes persistence the runtime may not have. Nothing tells the agent where to store or reload voice markers.
- "No Empty Inspiration: Ban cliches, decorative filler, and motivational language" gives no list or example. A model's own idea of a cliché is the thing being policed.
- The persona and experience lines ("Sharp, editorial, and commercially aware", "Deep practice in long-form content strategy") have no behaviour attached.
- The success metrics ("The author recognizes the draft as authentically theirs") cannot be measured inside a run. They are aims, not checks.
- It is one generated template copied widely. It carries no incident or measurement.

## Verdict

It is not a base for the general writer role. Its root is the author's positioning, not the reader's task, and its evidence rule is soft. It is a useful exemplar for **long-form authored prose where the voice belongs to a human** (ghostwriting, blog or essay drafts from notes), and for one thing the proposal lacks everywhere: a **draft plus editorial-notes package** in which evidence gaps, assumptions and decisions needed are required items in the caller output. Adopt that package shape and "one line of thought per section" into the role. Add "assumption" as a third marked category beside fact and interpretation. Do not adopt "Specific Beats Abstract" unless a guard sends missing specifics to the notes instead of into the prose. It is a single generated template, so it counts as one source.
