## What it is for and who reads its output

It revises a chapter that the Evaluator has scored, fixing the issues listed in the evaluation report while keeping what the report marks as strengths. Its outputs are the revised chapter (same file) and a revision report. The chapter's reader is the book reader. The report's reader is the Evaluator on the next round and the Orchestrator, and the report ends with "## Concerns for Re-evaluation", addressed to that reviewer.

## Operating procedure in brief

1. Read the evaluation report, the Disruptor's report, the strengths-to-preserve list, the chapter, foundation.md, the voice bank, the previous chapter, and `research/bestseller-dna.md` if present.
2. Write a revision plan before touching prose.
3. Fix in a fixed order: structural, then connective, then prose, then factual. Each level has a stated risk and a re-check to run after it.
4. Follow seven surgical principles, including minimum change, voice preservation, no unrequested improvements, and a burden of proof for deletion.
5. For each named evaluation issue, follow its playbook (voice drift, dialogue subtext, 20 AI patterns, emotional peak, commercial pacing, re-readability and others).
6. Overwrite the chapter with an updated header, and write `evaluations/revision-chapter-[N]-r[revision-number].md` with an issue table, strength verification, changes not made, and concerns for re-evaluation.

## What it has that the proposed writer lacks (verbatim)

- An order of operations that prevents wasted work: "Always fix problems top-down. Fixing prose before fixing structure = polishing a passage that will be deleted." The four levels (structural, connective, prose, factual) apply to revising any doc type, and each carries a re-check scope, for example "**Risk:** HIGH. Structural changes cascade. After any structural edit, re-read the entire chapter." The proposal has no model of revision at all; it covers drafting and merging.
- An explicit guard against the reviser's voice replacing the author's: "Your #1 risk is introducing YOUR voice while fixing THEIR text. After every edit, re-read against the voice bank." The proposal's merge mode combines claims from several drafts but does not say whose voice survives.
- A burden of proof on deletion, the opposite default to the proposal's point 3: "**Burden of proof for deletion:** Before removing anything, complete this sentence: \"This passage does nothing — not texture, not voice beat, not pacing buffer, not character noise.\" If you can name even a minor function, make a targeted fix, not a deletion." Paired with "Before deleting or converting any passage, name what it IS doing."
- Scope held to the brief: "If the evaluation doesn't flag something, don't \"improve\" it. You are not the writer. You are not the evaluator. You fix what's broken. You leave what works." and "Make the smallest change that fixes the problem."
- Respect for another agent's intentional changes: "**Do NOT undo disruptions unless the Evaluator specifically flagged them as harmful.** The Disruptor's changes are intentional — they break predictability." A general writer given drafts from several agents needs the same rule: some oddities are decisions, not errors.
- A revision ledger, one row per issue, tracing each change to its reason: "| # | Issue (from evaluation) | Type | Fix Applied | Strength Impact |". This is the proposal's claim-to-source ledger applied to edits: every change points back to the evaluation item that required it.
- A section for what was left undone and why: "## Changes Not Made (and Why)" with "[Issue]: [Why you didn't fix it — needs structural work beyond your scope, etc.]". The proposal's ledger lists kept claims; it does not say to list rejected or unaddressed ones.
- Judgment handed forward instead of claimed: "## Concerns for Re-evaluation" / "[Anything the Evaluator should specifically check in the next round]". This matches the proposal's rule that the writer does not judge its own output, and gives the next reviewer a place to start.
- A consistency sweep for one-off errors: "For consistency errors: grep the full manuscript for all occurrences and fix ALL of them." The same applies to renamed terms in project docs.
- Recognition that editing by an LLM introduces its own defects: "Your revisions must not INTRODUCE AI patterns. This is the #1 risk of LLM editing." followed by named checks ("Check for forced symmetry in your new text", "Check for rule of three", "Check for excessive em dashes").

## Where it conflicts with the proposal

- Point 3 (cutting is the default): directly opposed for text the agent did not write. The burden-of-proof rule and "**Deliberately rough sentences** — rhythmically off, grammatically imperfect. They ground the prose. If you can't tell if intentional, leave it." protect material a cut-first writer would remove. The prose level does ask for cuts ("Cut verbal fat: \"very,\" \"really,\" \"quite,\" \"somewhat,\" \"a bit,\" \"kind of\""), and "CVI-Launch" pacing fixes say "Check: are there paragraphs that can be CUT without losing meaning? Cut them.", so the rule is not to avoid cutting but to cut words freely and cut passages only with a named reason. That is a narrower and probably better version of the proposal's point 3 for revision work.
- Point 2 (specific over vague): one AI-pattern fix pushes the other way: "Precision Flex → Replace unnecessary exact numbers with vague human language where appropriate." For fiction voice this is right; for reports and project docs it is the opposite of what the proposal wants. It is a doc-type rule, not a role rule.
- Point 1 (the reader): the reader appears only through proxies the Evaluator defines ("Tomorrow Test", "Discovery Test", "Residue Test", "CVI-Launch", "CVI-Legacy"). The editor serves the evaluation report, not the reader directly. In a pipeline with a separate evaluator this is by design.
- Judging its own output: "## Strengths Verification" asks the editor to mark each strength "[preserved / enhanced / degraded — explain if degraded]", which is self-assessment. The "Concerns for Re-evaluation" section keeps the final call with the Evaluator.

## Verdict

It is the strongest exemplar in this batch for a revision or editing mode, and most of its core is not specific to fiction. The procedural core is its top-down revision order with re-check scope per level, minimum effective change, the guard against the reviser's voice, the burden of proof for deleting passages, no unrequested improvements, and respect for other agents' deliberate changes. Its report has an issue-to-fix table, changes not made, and concerns for the next reviewer. That core would transfer to revising project docs, reports or prompts, and it fits the proposal's ledger and its rule that runs, not the writer, judge the output. If the general writer gets an editing mode, this file is the base for it. The fiction playbooks (chaos, CVI, engagement types, the 20 AI patterns) belong in a fiction path rule or a fiction role together with c00076. Its tension with the proposal's cut-by-default is useful. It suggests a refinement: cut words by default, and cut passages only with a named reason, especially in text the writer did not write. That refinement should be tested in runs before it is adopted.
